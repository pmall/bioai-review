# ProteinMPNN and LigandMPNN

Source papers:
- **ProteinMPNN**: Dauparas, Anishchenko, Bennett et al., *Robust deep learning based protein sequence design using ProteinMPNN*, Science 378(6615):49–56, 2022.
- **LigandMPNN**: Dauparas, Lee, Pecoraro et al., *Atomic context-conditioned protein sequence design using LigandMPNN*, Nature Methods 22:717–723, 2025.

Both from the Baker Lab / Institute for Protein Design, UW. This document consolidates the goal, input/output representations, training mechanics, and use cases of both models, plus clarifications worked out in discussion.

---

## The problem these models solve

**Inverse folding**: given a fixed 3D protein backbone structure (already generated by some other method — Rosetta, RFdiffusion, hallucination, or simply an existing native structure), find an amino acid sequence likely to fold into that exact structure.

This is a strictly separate step from structure generation. Neither model ever proposes, modifies, or predicts 3D structure. **Everything geometric is input; the sequence is the only output.** (LigandMPNN also outputs candidate side-chain conformations — see below — but this is a downstream/optional add-on, not the core generative act.)

Both are **discriminative/generative sequence models**, not diffusion models. They involve no noising/denoising process at inference time. The "noise" mentioned in both papers is a training-time-only regularization trick (see Training section) and is architecturally unrelated to diffusion.

Neither model has a first-class notion of "target" as an architectural category. There is no dedicated target-encoder pathway and no asymmetry in how a binding partner vs. the chain-being-designed is treated by the network itself. "Target" only emerges as a *usage pattern*: certain parts of the input are treated as fixed/known (their true identity is supplied rather than predicted), while other parts are left for the model to fill in. This is a scheduling/masking convention, not a distinct model concept — a real, meaningful difference from generative structure models like the RFdiffusion family, where "how is the target represented" is a first-class architectural question.

## ProteinMPNN

### What it takes as input

- **Backbone coordinates only**: N, Cα, C, O for every residue of every chain in the structure. No side-chain atoms, no chemistry, no non-protein atoms.
- A **virtual Cβ** is computed geometrically from N/Cα/C (fixed trigonometric formula, not a real measured atom) and used as an additional reference point.
- **No sequence is given to the encoder.** The backbone encoder never sees amino acid identity — only geometry.
- **Multi-chain aware**: chains are labeled, and a same-chain/different-chain binary feature plus a relative positional encoding (residue-index offset, capped at ±32) let the model handle complexes without being sensitive to how chains are ordered.

### Graph structure

- Residues are nodes. Each node connects only to its **32 nearest neighbors** by Cα–Cα distance (sparse graph, not all-to-all) — this is what makes the model tractable and is justified by the observation that local geometric neighborhoods primarily determine residue identity (unlike the *structure prediction* problem, which needs long-range information).
- Edge features: the 25 pairwise distances between {N, Cα, C, O, virtual Cβ} of residue *i* and residue *j*, encoded via radial basis functions, plus the positional encoding described above.

### How the target (another protein, a binding partner, etc.) is represented

There is no separate "target" data type. A second protein in the complex — e.g., PD-L1 in a binder-design problem — is simply **another chain in the same multi-chain backbone graph**, encoded with the identical machinery as the chain being designed: backbone atoms only (N, Cα, C, O), no side chains.

Its **true, already-known sequence** is supplied as fixed context via the decoding-order mechanism (see below): the target chain's positions are "decoded" first, but instead of sampling from the predicted distribution, the real known identity is simply plugged in. This makes the target's real sequence visible as context for every subsequent decoding step on the chain actually being designed. So a protein target = full backbone geometry + real full sequence, both given as fixed input; only the new chain's residues are actually unknown.

### Output

- A categorical distribution over 21 classes (20 amino acids + 1 unknown/masking token) at each position.
- Sampling is autoregressive: one position decided at a time, each conditioned on all previously-decided positions (real, for fixed/target positions; sampled, for newly generated ones).
- No 3D output — ProteinMPNN produces sequence only.
- **Symmetric / multi-chain / repeat-protein design**: implemented purely at the output layer. For positions meant to be identical (e.g., equivalent residues across the subunits of a homo-oligomer, or repeats within a repeat protein), the predicted logits for all tied positions are **averaged before sampling**, producing one shared distribution from which a single amino acid is drawn and applied everywhere at once. The encoder/graph itself has no notion of symmetry — it's entirely a decode-time trick.
- **Multi-state design**: logits from multiple target backbone states can be linearly combined (with positive/negative weights) before sampling, to upweight or downweight sequences favoring one state over another.

### Why decoding order matters, and why it's randomized

A protein sequence has a chemical N-to-C direction, but there's no reason position 1's identity needs to be decided before position 2's — in the folded structure, all residues coexist and influence each other simultaneously. Early sequence-design models nonetheless copied the language-model convention of strict left-to-right (N-to-C) autoregressive decoding. This is a real limitation: if part of the sequence is already known/fixed (e.g., a target chain, or a fixed peptide motif in the middle of the designed chain), a strict N-to-C model cannot use that known information to inform residues that come "before" it in sequence order.

ProteinMPNN's fix: train the model on **randomly sampled decoding orders** rather than a fixed one, so that at inference time an order can be *chosen* for the task at hand — e.g., decode all fixed/known positions first, so their (real) identities are available as context for every position that still needs to be designed.

**Mechanically, how a single random order is realized:**

For a given forward pass, a permutation of the L positions is sampled. From that permutation, a causal mask over all position pairs (i, j) is built: mask[i,j] = 1 if j precedes i in this order, 0 otherwise. At each decoder layer, two parallel versions of every edge message are computed — one including the neighbor's amino acid identity (real, if fixed context; previously-sampled, if generated), and one with identity information zeroed out (geometry only). The causal mask selects, for each directed edge i→j, which version position i is allowed to see: if j comes before i in the order, i sees j's identity; if j comes after, i sees only j's structure, with no leakage of future information.

**Training**: because the true sequence is fully known during training, this does *not* require actually decoding sequentially. A random permutation is sampled, the mask is built accordingly, and a **single forward pass** computes the correct conditional probability at every position simultaneously — the same style as teacher-forcing in a left-to-right language model, except "left-to-right" is replaced by a different random order sampled at every training step. The loss is per-position cross-entropy against the true residue, given whatever conditioning that step's random order and mask permit.

**Inference**: no ground truth exists, so decoding genuinely is sequential here — an order is chosen (deliberately, for the task), one position is decoded and sampled, its sampled identity is fed back in as now-known context, the next position is decoded, and so on. This sequential loop is what allows deliberately decoding fixed positions first: for a complex with a known chain (a target protein, a fixed motif) alongside a chain being designed, the order is chosen so the known chain's real, already-known identities are decoded first and plugged in directly (no sampling needed), making that full known sequence available as context for every position on the chain still being generated.

**Why random order is viable for a protein sequence but would break natural language.** This is not the same operation as masked language modeling (BERT-style), which predicts all masked positions *simultaneously and independently* of each other, given only the visible/unmasked context. ProteinMPNN's decoding is still fully autoregressive — one position at a time, each one conditioning on every position decided so far, including other positions that started out equally unknown. "Random order" just means the factorization order of the autoregressive chain rule is randomized at training time instead of fixed to left-to-right; the chain rule itself is valid under *any* ordering of the same variables, so this is a legitimate reparameterization, not an approximation.

The reason this is harmless for protein sequence (but would be destructive for English text) is that a protein's linear N-to-C numbering is largely bookkeeping: which amino acid belongs at a given position is determined by that position's **3D neighborhood**, and that neighborhood is captured directly as geometric edge features (Cα–Cα and other backbone distances) — a channel that is completely independent of, and never hidden by, decoding order. A relative-position feature (chain-index offset between residues i and j, capped at ±32) is also always present as an edge feature, entirely separately from decoding order — so the model always knows how far apart two residues are along the chain, regardless of which one is decided first. In English, by contrast, word order *is* the primary carrier of meaning (subject/object relationships, syntax) — there is no separate, order-independent "spatial" structure underneath a sentence the way there is for a folded protein, so randomizing the order in which words are decided would destroy the only channel that encodes those relationships.

**A real consequence worth naming: neighbor identity can be genuinely unknown at decode time.** For any given sampled order, a position's closest 3D neighbors may well be decoded *after* it — e.g., if position 40's nearest neighbors in the 32-neighbor graph are positions 12, 41, 88, and 103, and the random order happens to visit 40 first, none of those neighbors' amino acid identities are available yet when 40 is decided. However, only *identity* is masked by the causal mechanism — geometry is never masked. The full backbone is encoded once, up front, over the entire fixed structure, so position 40 always has complete access to the exact 3D arrangement of all its neighbors (distances, burial, pocket shape, proximity to a ligand) regardless of whether their amino acid identities are known yet. This is consistent with the ablation evidence (Table 1): switching to random-order decoding produced only a small recovery gain (~0.3–0.8 points) on top of the much larger gain already provided by geometric distance features alone — suggesting neighbor *identity* is a real but secondary signal compared to neighbor *geometry*, which is unconditionally available in every decoding order.

### Use cases

- **Monomer design**: one backbone in, sequence out. No other context.
- **Binder / protein-protein interface design**: designed chain's backbone + target chain's backbone and real sequence, both in the same multi-chain graph; only the designed chain's residues are sampled.
- **Symmetric assemblies / cyclic oligomers**: tied positions across copies via logit averaging.
- **Repeat protein design**: tied positions within a chain (each repeat unit constrained to match).
- **Combined symmetry**: tying both within-chain (repeat) and between-chain (cyclic) positions simultaneously.
- **Multi-state design**: a single sequence intended to be compatible with two or more distinct backbone states.
- **Rescuing failed designs**: keeping a previously-generated backbone (from Rosetta or AlphaFold-hallucination) fixed and simply re-running sequence design with ProteinMPNN — the paper's central experimental validation strategy.

## LigandMPNN

### What it adds over ProteinMPNN

The ability to condition sequence design on **non-protein atoms**: small molecules, nucleotides (DNA/RNA), and metal ions. ProteinMPNN's backbone-only pathway is fully retained and unchanged; LigandMPNN adds a second, parallel pathway alongside it.

### Two separate input pathways for "other stuff around the protein being designed"

**Pathway 1 — other protein chains (inherited from ProteinMPNN, unmodified).** If a target in the complex is itself a protein, it goes through the exact same protein-graph mechanism described above: backbone atoms only (N, Cα, C, O + virtual Cβ), multi-chain aware, with its real known sequence supplied via the decoding-order trick. LigandMPNN does not extend or replace this pathway.

**Pathway 2 — the ligand-atom pathway (new in LigandMPNN).** A bag of atoms, each carrying only:
- a fixed, real, never-predicted **3D coordinate**, and
- a **one-hot chemical element type** (further embedded via that element's periodic-table group and period, which is why the model can partially generalize to rare elements by proximity in the periodic table rather than requiring exact prior exposure).

Critically, **no explicit bond/connectivity graph is given** — the only relational information among ligand atoms is geometric proximity. The model has to infer "these atoms form one connected molecule" purely from distances, not from any chemical-bond annotation.

This pathway is atom-type-agnostic by construction: nothing in its design restricts it to non-protein atoms. The paper explicitly exploits this — during training, 2–4% of protein residues had their side-chain atoms randomly routed through the ligand pathway as extra fixed "ligand" context, to allow a user to pin an exact functional/catalytic side-chain atom position (e.g., "this histidine's side-chain nitrogen must be exactly here") as rigid geometric context, distinct from treating that residue as part of a normal protein chain with its own sequence.

**Rule of thumb**: if the other entity is a whole protein chain that should be treated as such (own sequence, own backbone) → protein pathway. If it's a fixed geometric fact you want the model to see, regardless of its chemical origin (small molecule, metal, nucleotide, or even a specific protein atom) → ligand pathway.

### Graph structure (three graphs total)

1. **Protein-only graph** — identical to ProteinMPNN's.
2. **Intra-ligand graph** — for each residue, its **25 closest ligand atoms** (by distance to that residue's virtual Cβ) are connected to each other in a fully-connected local subgraph, letting information flow between ligand atoms before reaching the protein.
3. **Protein–ligand graph** — connects each residue to its nearest ligand atoms; edges encode distances between {N, Cα, C, O, virtual Cβ} and each ligand atom, plus angle-based sin/cos features describing ligand atoms in the frame of the residue's N–Cα–C plane.

These are processed by dedicated encoder blocks (a protein–ligand encoder, two message-passing layers) whose output is combined with the ordinary protein-encoder representations before decoding. Because the ligand context is only pulled in per-residue within a local neighborhood (not globally), only nearby ligand atoms influence a given residue's sidechain identity/conformation — consistent with the physical short-range nature of the relevant interactions (van der Waals, electrostatics, solvation).

### Output

- Same autoregressive categorical sequence output as ProteinMPNN (21 classes, random decoding order, causal masking exactly as described above) — LigandMPNN did not change this mechanism, only what conditioning information is available to it.
- **New**: a separate downstream **sidechain-packing network**, using the same core architecture, that takes the now-fixed designed sequence (plus backbone and any ligand context) and predicts full sidechain conformations — the four chi torsion angles per residue. Each chi angle is modeled as a **mixture of three von Mises (circular normal) distributions**, decoded autoregressively (chi1 first, then chi2 conditioned on chi1's realized 3D position, then chi3, then chi4). This lets a user actually inspect whether a designed sidechain plausibly points toward the ligand in the intended geometry, not just infer it from the identity label.
- Sidechain conformations can also be supplied as **input** (rather than only produced as output), enabling explicit stabilization of a functional site by fixing the position of chosen protein sidechain atoms.

### Use cases

- **Small-molecule binder / sensor design**: fixed rigid ligand pose (as coordinates only, no bonds) + backbone → sequence and sidechain packing optimized for pocket interactions.
- **Enzyme active-site design**: similar, typically combined with pinning specific catalytic side-chain atoms via the ligand pathway's atom-agnostic mechanism.
- **DNA/nucleotide-binding protein design**: nucleotide atoms (bases, backbone) treated as ligand-pathway context; used, e.g., for designing sequence-specific major-groove DNA readers.
- **Metal-binding site design**: a metal ion is simply a single ligand atom (one coordinate, one element label) — the same generic pathway, no special-casing.
- **Rescuing/improving weak or non-binding designs**: as in ProteinMPNN, backbone (and here, ligand) held fixed from a previous failed Rosetta design, sequence redesigned with LigandMPNN — used experimentally to rescue a non-binding rocuronium design and to substantially improve a weak cholic-acid binder's affinity.

## Training (both models)

- Trained on **PDB biological assemblies** (X-ray/cryo-EM, resolution better than 3.5 Å), sequences clustered at 30% identity to define train/test splits, avoiding near-duplicate structures leaking across the split.
- **Loss**: standard categorical cross-entropy on the true amino acid at each position, computed in a single forward pass per training example thanks to the random-permutation/causal-mask trick described above (no need to actually decode step-by-step during training).
- **Backbone (and, for LigandMPNN, ligand) coordinate noise is added only during training** (Gaussian, small standard deviation — e.g. 0.02–0.1 Å depending on experiment): this is a regularization measure, not a generative diffusion process. Two reasons given: (1) it prevents the model from exploiting fine-grained artifacts of crystallographic refinement that subtly encode the native sequence in the coordinates themselves (a form of information leakage / label memorization specific to real crystal structures), and (2) it makes the model robust to the fact that at real design time, input backbones (from Rosetta, hallucination, or diffusion models) are imperfect approximations, not atomically exact crystal structures.
- There is a direct trade-off, confirmed for both models: more training noise **decreases** raw native-sequence recovery on unperturbed crystal structures, but **increases** the practical usefulness of the model — sequences from noised models are more confidently and accurately refolded by AlphaFold in single-sequence mode, and are more useful for actual downstream design (since the real goal is "does it fold as intended," not "does it match the PDB residue exactly").
- LigandMPNN specifically retrained ProteinMPNN on its own (identical) dataset for fair comparison, since the original 2022 ProteinMPNN was trained on a different, non-overlapping dataset — so ProteinMPNN numbers quoted in the LigandMPNN paper are not directly comparable to the original 2022 paper's headline 52.4% figure; they are a different, matched baseline.

## Standing criticisms (earned, not exhaustive)

- **All comparisons are intra-family plus Rosetta.** Neither paper benchmarks against other groups' deep-learning inverse-folding methods (e.g., ESM-IF) or non-Baker-lab atomic-context sequence design tools, limiting conclusions about standing relative to the field as a whole.
- **ProteinMPNN's "rescue" framing** means most headline wet-lab numbers are measured against a weak baseline (designs that had already failed under Rosetta or hallucination), not against Rosetta's successes — a real and useful demonstration, but not symmetric evidence of general superiority on backbones Rosetta already handles well.
- **LigandMPNN's most quoted wet-lab validation figure (">100 interfaces, 5 co-crystal structures")** is a roll-up of separate companion papers, not data generated within this paper itself; the paper's own directly-shown wet-lab cases are limited to two rescue examples (rocuronium, cholic acid).
- **Sidechain packing chi3/chi4 accuracy remains poor across all methods** (roughly 20–28% and 8–19% respectively for LigandMPNN vs. Rosetta) — claims about "detailed evaluation of binding interactions" via generated sidechains are best read as applying mainly to chi1/chi2 (the coarse direction a sidechain points), not exact terminal rotamer placement.
- LigandMPNN's ablation showing sidechain-packing improvement over "LigandMPNN-wo" (no ligand context) is much smaller than its improvement over Rosetta, indicating **most sidechain packing accuracy comes from protein backbone context (preorganization) rather than from seeing the ligand directly** — a finding the paper reports transparently rather than downplaying.
