## **Abstract** 

Deep learning has revolutionized biomolecular modeling, enabling the prediction of diverse structures with atomic accuracy. However, leveraging the atomic-level precision of the structure prediction model for de novo design remains challenging. Here, we present HalluDesign, a general all-atom framework for protein optimization and de novo design, which iteratively update protein structure and sequence. HalluDesign harnesses the inherent hallucination capabilities of AlphaFold3-style structure prediction models and enables fine-tune free, forward-pass only sequence-structure co-optimization. Structure conditioning at different noise level in the structure prediction stage allows precise control over the sampling space, facilitating tasks from local and global protein optimization to de novo design. We demonstrate the versatility of this approach by optimizing suboptimal structures, rescuing previously unsuccessful designs, designing new biomolecular interactions and generating new protein structures from scratch. Experimental characterization of binder design spanning small molecule, metal ion, protein, and antibody design of phosphorylation-specific peptide revealed high design success rates and excellent structural accuracy. Together, our comprehensive computational and experimental results highlight the broad utility of this framework. We anticipate that HalluDesign will further unlock the modeling and design potential of AlphaFold3-like models, enabling the systematic creation of complex proteins for a wide range of biotechnological applications. 

## **Introduction** 

Deep learning–based neural networks for protein structure prediction have revolutionized the field, enabling the computational modeling of protein structures with atomic accuracy primarily using sequence information<sup>1-4</sup> . Nevertheless, harnessing the atomic-level precision of these state-of-the-art models into a robust framework for de novo design—rather than just prediction—remains a significant challenge<sup>5</sup> . Recent progress in protein design has leveraged the pretrained structure prediction models<sup>6-</sup> 9, which can be broadly classified into two categories: gradient-based backpropagation methods and finetuning approaches. Gradient-based methods iteratively update sequence and structure compatibility by minimizing network loss<sup>10-12</sup> ; however, they typically require high computational costs and often fail to

In contrast, the fine-tuning approach enable models to generate protein backbones from random noise with forward pass only<sup>13-16</sup> . However, a large number of scaffolds and subsequent sequence sampling are necessary in order to identify sufficient candidates that pass in silico structure prediction filters such as AlphaFold2 or AlphaFold3, prior to experimental characterization. 

Recently, AlphaFold3 has extended structure prediction beyond proteins to include small molecules, nucleic acids, ions, and covalent modifications, achieving unprecedented accuracy<sup>17,18</sup> . This breakthrough, along with the various open-source reproductions it inspired<sup>19-21</sup> , has spurred a new wave of adaptation and fine-tuning for the modeling and design of diverse biomolecules<sup>22-25</sup> . The AlphaFold3 architecture is primarily composed of the pairformer module, which extracts residue/atom-level and pairwise representations from input features, and a diffusion module, which generates all-atom structures from Gaussian noise, guided by the information extracted from the pairformer. This generative diffusion process inherently leads to "hallucination" - the generation of plausible-looking structures even in the low-confidence regions ( Fig. 1a)<sup>17,18</sup> . While this property is an undesirable side-effect for structure prediction, we hypothesized that it could be repurposed for structure optimization and de novo protein design, without requiring fine-tuning or computationally intensive backpropagation. Here, we introduce HalluDesign—a hallucination-driven, forward-pass-only framework that exploits the structure module of AlphaFold3-style models to iteratively optimize and design both protein sequences and structures. 

## **HalluDesign methodology** 

The real protein sequence–structure landscape is highly rugged<sup>26-28</sup> , making it challenging to find optimal solutions through iterative traversing the sequence and structure space. We reasoned that diffusion-based structure prediction models inherently smooth this rugged landscape, which is often observed as ‘hallucination’ effect (Fig. 1b). To harness this property for optimization and de novo design, we developed HalluDesign, an iterative framework designed to co-optimize protein sequence and structure (Fig. 1c), alternating between a structure update stage and a sequence design stage. 

This iterative framework begins with the structure update stage, where the diffusion module of an AlphaFold3-style model leverages its 'hallucination' capabilities to refine the protein structure, guiding it towards a more ideal state conditioned on an input sequence. This process allows plausible conformations to be generated even for suboptimal proteins that lack stable folds and interactions. In the subsequent sequence design stage, an inverse folding deep learning model, such as ProteinMPNN or LigandMPNN<sup>29,30</sup> , generates a new amino acid sequence highly compatible with the updated structure. This new sequence is then fed back into the next structure update iteration, serving to better condition the diffusion module and ensure the model operates with a more compatible sequence and structure pair. Crucially, the entire process involves only forward passes through the structure prediction and sequence design models, requiring neither model fine-tuning nor computationally intensive loss backpropagation. The built-in confidence metrics of the structure prediction model, such as pLDDT or ipTM, further provide intrinsic, real-time indicators of the optimization's progress (Supplementary Figs. S8). 

The structure update stage provides a unified and controllable strategy by utilizing a truncated diffusion approach. Instead of initializing the diffusion process of the structure generation module of AlphaFold3 with random Gaussian noise, a user-defined noise level is applied to the initial or a previous iteration's

This method offers precise control over the structural exploration by truncating the diffusion trajectory at various timesteps. Shorter trajectories, corresponding to lower noise levels, prompt local refinement, which is well-suited for optimization tasks requiring the preservation of overall topology. Conversely, longer trajectories, corresponding to higher noise levels, enable broader exploration of structure space to escape local minima (Fig. 2a). 

Following structure update, the complementary sequence design stage is performed, which is substantially more computationally efficient than the structure update step. First, we employ efficient inverse folding models, such as ProteinMPNN or LigandMPNN, to sample a set of candidate sequences for the updated protein backbone. These sequences are then ranked by CoDP (Contrastive-learning-based Distogram Prediction Model), a fast and lightweight tool we developed to evaluate sequence compatibility with the input structure through distogram prediction and contrastive learning<sup>1,3,4,31,32</sup> . The top-ranked sequence is then selected and serves as the conditioned sequence input for the next structure update stage. The CoDP model was trained on structures from the AlphaFold Database (AFDB)<sup>32</sup> . For each structure, multiple sequences are sampled using ProteinMPNN. CoDP was then trained to distinguish these synthetic sequences, learning to identify those that yield high-confidence predictions in AlphaFold2 and remain structurally consistent with the input backbone, from those sequences that do not. 

## **HalluDesign for protein optimization** 

While state-of-the-art generative models typically use AlphaFold3 as an external 'oracle' to select highquality candidates from a large set of designs, we hypothesized that HalluDesign could instead actively refine these existing designs. We first applied HalluDesign to optimize monomeric scaffolds generated by Chroma<sup>33</sup> . Both structural and confidence metrics improved steadily across iterative cycles (Supplementary Figs. S1). We observed distinct optimization behaviors by tuning the diffusion trajectory length: shorter trajectories (e.g., 50 steps) preserved the input topology but converged more slowly, making them ideal for refinement. Longer trajectories (100–200 steps) explored broader structural landscapes and reached high-confidence solutions rapidly, typically within 6–7 cycles (Supplementary Figs. S1). This trade-off was more pronounced for large proteins (>300 aa), which required longer trajectories or additional cycles to accommodate the substantial conformational changes needed for convergence. 

To further test the generality of HalluDesign's optimization capabilities, we applied it to scaffolds generated by RSO<sup>34</sup> , a state-of-the-art backpropagation-based AlphaFold2 design method. We selected RSO as a challenging test case because it is recognized as one of the most performant methods for monomer generation, particularly for large proteins. Remarkably, our forward-pass-only approach further improved their computational metrics, suggesting that the conformational landscape of AlphaFold3, which HalluDesign leverages, is smoother and more amenable to optimization than the AF2 landscape traversed by gradient-based methods (Supplementary Figs. S2). In contrast, we observed that RFdiffusion partial diffusion were unable to refine these same RSO-generated scaffolds<sup>16,35</sup> . This highlights that protein structure landscape is more accurately modeled by AF3 than that of RFdiffusion, underscoring the necessity of leveraging AF3 as the foundational model for optimization.

Having demonstrated HalluDesign's efficacy on monomers, we next tested its ability to refine biomolecular interactions. We first applied HalluDesign to protein binders generated by RFdiffusion from their published PPI benchmark set, which were under experimental evaluation and already exhibit high confidence metrics. Remarkably, HalluDesign further improved both structural accuracy and confidence across all targets (Fig. 2c). Similarly, structure conditioning with shorter diffusion trajectories achieved comparable optimization performance to longer ones, while better preserving structural integrity. Then we leveraged the all-atom capabilities of the underlying AF3 model by optimizing smallmolecule binders. We generated initial binders for a diverse set of ligands using the RFdiffusion all-atom pipeline<sup>36</sup> , which shows modest model quality by AF3 metrics. Applying HalluDesign significantly improved the computational metrics across all targets. This enhancement was particularly pronounced for large, polar ligands with many rotatable bonds (Supplementary Figs. S5-7). We attribute this success to HalluDesign's ability to co-optimize both the protein structure and the ligand conformation simultaneously—a distinct advantage over the fixed-ligand approach used by the original generative mode which is absent in the initial, fixed-ligand generation process. 

## **HalluDesign for scaffold-based de novo protein design** 

Beyond refining existing structures, we hypothesized that HalluDesign could design new interaction with targets using existing protein scaffolds that do not naturally interact with targets. To test this, we performed de novo design of small molecule binders on two distinct topologies. First, we used the nuclear transport factor 2 (NTF2) scaffold<sup>7,37</sup> , a compact and engineerable fold known to accommodate diverse ligands. Second, to verify the generality of this approach, we used a large set of pseudocycle scaffolds with pockets aligned along their pseudo-symmetric axis (Fig. 3a)<sup>38</sup> . For both scaffold types, our approach involved placing a randomly selected ligand conformer at the center of the protein—a strategy we term "black hole initialization"—and applying the HalluDesign co-optimization loop (Supplementary Figs. S9-10). Moreover, we truncated the diffusion step to 150 unless otherwise specified for design, as this configuration provided similar optimization performance to the default 200-step diffusion trajectory while reducing computational consumption. 

Despite the initial ligands placements for both NTF2 and pseudocycle scaffolds lacking any established protein-ligand interactions, HalluDesign simultaneously co-optimized ligand poses, protein side chains, and protein-ligand interactions, converging on plausible complex conformations with high in-silico success rates. This success rate was inversely proportional to ligand size and polarity: for small, hydrophobic ligands, the rate exceeded 60% (Supplementary Figs. S9), while it was considerably lower for larger, polar targets. This direct co-optimization approach bypasses the need for initial docking steps commonly used with previous methods<sup>39</sup> . Furthermore, HalluDesign achieved significantly higher in silico success rates on these tasks compared to both diffusion-based models like RFdiffusion all-atom and backpropagation methods like BoltzDesign1. 

Antibody and nanobody design remain challenging due to flexible loop-dominated interfaces, and we hypothesized that HalluDesign could leverage AlphaFold3’s strong performance on antibody–protein complexes to facilitate CDR loop reconstruction. The antibody framework is an ideal predefined scaffold platform for targeting a vast diversity of molecular interfaces task, as it provides a highly conserved and general scaffold that presents its hypervariable loops (CDRs) (Fig. 3b). ## **HalluDesign for from scratch protein design** 

We next explored another challenging application: de novo protein design from scratch. This general strategy initiates the designed protein with pure Gaussian noise as its coordinates and a random protein sequence, without any structure input (Fig. 3c). After a single prediction pass generates an initial, plausible conformation, this structure is then fed into our standard HalluDesign cycles. We first applied this approach to design cyclic peptide binders against protein targets. This application involved two modifications to the general strategy: the target protein structure was provided as a template, and we modified the peptide's position index in AF3 to enforce a head-to-tail cyclization<sup>8,41,42</sup> (Supplementary Figs. S13). Leveraging AF3's unique ability to model covalent modifications, this framework also allowed us to incorporate non-natural amino acids (NCAAs)—either to form covalent bonds with a target cysteine or to engineer side-chain-to-side-chain cyclization which broader explore the design space<sup>40,43</sup> (Supplementary Figs. S14). 

However, when this de novo strategy was applied to proteins substantially larger than peptides, the AlphaFold3-based framework failed. The optimization process consistently collapsed into lowcomplexity conformations, such as a single long helix. We hypothesized this is because the random starting point lies too far from the learned manifold of natural proteins, preventing the optimization from finding a convergence path (Fig. 3d). To overcome this, we substituted the AF3 engine with Protenix, an AF3-style architecture built on protein language model embeddings<sup>20</sup> . We reason that the rich contextual information from the language model effectively smooths this rugged landscape, allowing Protenix to find a convergence path even from such highly 'out-of-manifold' starting points. 

This capability for unconditional structure generation lays a solid foundation for overcoming the dependency on predefined scaffolds in designing new molecular interactions. By providing a target molecule as input—such as a protein, ligand, or nucleic acid—the Protenix based HalluDesign can directly leverage language model to interpret even random initial sequences, exploring plausible structural space and identifying conformations capable of forming possible interactions with the target. Consequently, Protenix-based HalluDesign enable the _de novo_ design of diverse biomolecular binders from scratch, harnessing the intrinsic sequence–structure knowledge embedded within language model– powered structure prediction frameworks. 

## **Experimental validation of protein-ligand interactions** 

We designed binders for a range of key endogenous small molecules characterized by multiple rotatable bonds and polar groups, including cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and adrenaline (AD). In addition, we targeted small molecules with relatively large, rigid, and hydrophobic features, such as cholic acid (CA) and the HIV drug dolutegravir (DTG). We also developed binders for the amino acid tryptophan (TRP) and extended our approach to metal ion ligand zinc (Zn²⁺).

These targets representing a broad spectrum of ligand sizes and chemical properties (Fig. 4a). NTF2 scaffolds were used for ATP, CA, DTG, and AD, while pseudocycle scaffolds or hybrid NTF2 scaffold were employed for cAMP, TRP and Zn²⁺. We initialized each protein-ligand complex structure using the “black hole initialization” strategy and applied HalluDesign to generate the final models. Unlike conventional high-throughput design approaches that require screening large numbers of candidates<sup>38,44</sup> , HalluDesign achieved binder discovery in a low-throughput setting. For each small-molecule target, fewer than 16 designed proteins were experimentally characterized, most of which were successfully expressed in _E. coli_ . ITC measurements confirmed one or more binders for every target. Mutational analysis of the binding pockets confirmed that ligand binding occurs at the intended site and that binding strength is highly dependent on the engineered interactions. Cross-binding assays demonstrated high specificity; for example, the TRP binder did not bind to the other aromatic amino acids, tyrosine or phenylalanine, highlighting the platform’s potential for designing amino acid-specific binders. Similarly, the AD binder exhibited only minimal binding to its metabolic product, norepinephrine (NE), which differs from AD by a single methyl group, underscoring the fine specificity of our designs. 

We successfully obtained protein-ligand complex crystal structures for cAMP, AD and Zn²⁺ (Fig. 4b). The cAMP-binder complex was resolved at 3.1 Å, with clear electron density for cAMP and an overall structure closely matching the design model (Cα RMSD of 0.74 Å for the protein and 1.07 Å for the ligand using the protein as reference). The sidechain configurations of pocket residues were also accurately recapitulated. Notably, all seven hydrogen bonds predicted in the design model between the protein and cAMP were observed in the crystal structure. The AD binder complex was resolved at 2.4 Å resolution, showing good overall agreement with the design model (Cα RMSD = 0.65 Å). In both the design model and the crystal structure, AD forms strong hydrogen-bonding interactions with protein binder. A leucine residue faces the methyl group, which explains the higher specificity for AD compared to NE. The zinc binder complex was resolved at 1.6 Å, with overall agreement with the design model (Cα RMSD of 0.81 Å). Zinc displayed tetrahedral coordination geometry in both the design model and the crystal structure, with all four coordinating residues precisely positioned. We also obtained apo structures for the DTG and TRP binders at 2.0 Å resolution, respectively, also in close atomic agreement with the design models (Cα RMSD of 0.31 Å and 0.81 Å). Pocket residue sidechain orientations in these structures mirrored those in the predicted models, further demonstrating atomic-level accuracy. The high experimental success rate, pocket point mutation analysis, and the atomic agreement between the crystal structures and design models showcase the versatility and accuracy of our platform for creating specific and efficient protein-ligand interactions. 

## **Experimental validation of protein-protein interactions** 

We next applied HalluDesign to rescue previously unsuccessful designs in the RFdiffusion protein– protein interaction (PPI) binder design task. In these experiments, we truncated the AlphaFold3 diffusion trajectory and conditioned structure prediction on either the initial complex structure or intermediate predictions from earlier optimization iterations. The resulting design models exhibited improved computational metrics for the binders, along with lower RMSD deviation from the original input structures. We selected 16 rescued designs each for PD-L1 and IL7RA for experimental validation, with each candidate derived from a previously failed design (Fig. 5a). Biolayer interferometry (BLI) assays demonstrated that 12 out of 16 PD-L1 binders successfully bound their target, while 8 out of 16 IL7RA binders showed binding to IL7RA. Targeting amyloid β (Aβ) with high specificity remains a crucial strategy in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) therapy<sup>45</sup> . Phosphorylation at Ser8 (pS8-Aβ) promotes toxic oligomer formation and accelerates aggregation, so designing antibodies that selectively recognize this pathogenic form could enable targeted intervention against the most neurotoxic Aβ species<sup>46</sup> . Using HalluDesign, we generated new CDR loops for the Herceptin scFv to interact specifically with phosphorylated Aβ. Unlike conventional methods that rely on predefined peptide positions or predefined target peptide second structure<sup>16,47</sup> , HalluDesign models free sampling of peptide conformations. Experimental results demonstrated specific binding to the phosphorylated form of Aβ, with no detectable interaction with native Aβ (Fig. 5b). These findings indicate that the phosphate group is critical for antibody-peptide interaction. This is consistent with our design model, where adjacent Arg residues near the phosphorylation site function as an electrostatic switch, ensuring specific binding to the only phosphorylated form of Aβ. 

We then applied Protenix-based HalluDesign to the de novo design of protein binders with biomedical relevance—specifically targeting PD-L1 and FGFR2, which are key proteins in immunotherapy and cancer development<sup>48,49</sup> . Starting from random binder sequences, Protenix-based HalluDesign produced models with strong computational metrics. The resulting binders interacted favorably with the beta sheet face of PD-L1 and FGFR2, demonstrating both high shape and chemical complementarity to the targets. For each target, we experimentally tested 16 de novo designed binders and observed high success rates: 8 out of 16 PD-L1 binders showed binding signals in BLI experiments, with the strongest affinity reaching 32 pM, and 13 out of 16 FGFR2 binders showed binding, with the best achieving digital nanomolar affinity (Fig. 5c). The impressive success rates and high binding affinities achieved by HalluDesign strongly demonstrate its power for de novo binder protein generation, highlighting its promise for developing binding reagents against a range of biomolecular targets. 

## **Conclusions** 

In summary, we present HalluDesign, a general all-atom framework that fundamentally reframes the utility of state-of-the-art structure prediction models. Instead of employing AlphaFold3-style models as passive, external 'oracles' to filter candidate designs, HalluDesign leverages the hallucination effect in their diffusion module for iterative sequence-structure optimization. Our approach smooths the rugged sequence-structure landscape, enabling rapid convergence without costly backpropagation or model finetuning. Furthermore, its variable noise conditioning enables a wide range of applications, from acting as a 'plug-in' tool to optimize any existing design to de novo design from scratch. 

We have demonstrated the broad versatility and atomic-level accuracy of this approach, achieving high experimental success rates in tasks ranging from optimizing existing designs to the de novo design of protein binders, small-molecule binders, and phosphorylation-specific antibodies. As structure prediction models continue to evolve, achieving higher accuracy across a wider range of molecular modalities, the performance of this hallucination-driven design strategy will be further enhanced, opening a wider exploration space for protein design based on these powerful prediction models.

### **Overview of the HalluDesign framework illustrating its principles, architecture, optimization strategies, and design pipeline. A-C** 

(A) Structure Hallucination serves as a potent structural optimization tool that can "hallucinate" ligand binding sites and artificially stabilize protein folds, thereby enabling more accurate and efficient design processes. 

(B) By smoothing the sequence–structure landscape, our approach minimizes the risk of getting trapped in local minima. This allows for the optimization of up to 1000 low-quality Chroma backbones into highquality, refined structures. 

(C) Overall Architecture of the HalluDesign Framework: The system is versatile, capable of handling a wide range of biomolecular targets. It can optionally integrate structure or template data, such as antibody frameworks or ligand-binding topologies. In the absence of templates, HalluDesign is fully capable of generating proteins from scratch.
